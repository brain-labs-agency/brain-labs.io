How to hire a bilingual SDR from Latin America

By the BL Agency team11 min readNovember 2, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Latin America works US hours. A rep in Lima or Bogotá covers a full Eastern day without a night shift.
  • The rate is the smallest part of the cost. Ramp time, management and a bad hire weigh far more.
  • The CV tells you little. A 20 minute screen with a surprise switch to English and a live roleplay tells you most of what you need.
  • Pick the seat first (SDR, cold caller, setter or AE), then the engagement model (contractor, direct hire or managed outbound).

Most US B2B companies reach the same point. Referrals slow down, the founder is still doing outbound at night, and a US SDR costs more than the pipeline can justify yet. Hiring a bilingual SDR from Latin America is the answer many of them land on, and for good reason.

It is also easy to get wrong. The candidates who look best on paper are not always the ones who can hold a call in English when a US buyer says no. This guide covers what to plan for beyond the rep, how to pick the right seat, how to test candidates quickly, and how to set the hire up to work.

01Why US companies hire sales reps in Latin America

If you sell to US companies, you need someone on the phone and in the inbox while your buyers are at their desks. That rules out most offshore regions unless you ask people to work through the night, and night shifts are where sales teams burn out first.

Latin America solves the hours problem. Lima and Bogotá sit on US Eastern time for most of the year. Mexico City is one hour behind, São Paulo two hours ahead. A rep in any of those cities can cover an Eastern or Central day on a normal schedule, and still overlap with a Pacific team for most of the afternoon.

The second reason is language. There is a deep pool of people across the region who have sold to US buyers in English for years: SaaS SDRs, call center team leads who moved into B2B, account executives at US startups with remote teams. Many of them can also run Spanish or Portuguese speaking accounts when you expand south.

The third reason is cost, and it is the one everybody leads with. It matters, but it is the weakest reason on its own. Companies that hire purely to save money tend to hire the cheapest candidate, skip the screen, and end up replacing the rep in month three. The companies that get the most out of the region hire for skill and hours, and treat the savings as a bonus that lets them hire two good people instead of one.

A final point that often gets missed: culture. Reps in the region grow up on US media and many have worked inside US sales processes, with the same CRMs, the same sequences and the same weekly pipeline reviews. The ramp is shorter than most managers expect, provided the onboarding is clear.

02What to budget for besides the rep

Cost is the reason most companies start looking at Latin America, and it is also where most of them make their first mistake: they compare one monthly rate with another and stop there. The rep's pay is only one line of what it takes to get qualified conversations on your calendar.

Ramp time. Even a strong rep needs a few weeks to learn your product, your buyer and the objections your closer hears. Plan for the first month to be about learning, not volume.

Management. Someone on your side has to review messages, listen to calls and run a weekly check-in. If nobody owns that, the best hire in the world will drift.

Tools and data. A sequencer, a dialer, contact data and a sending domain that is warmed up before the first email goes out. Ask your partner what is included and what is not.

Commission. Good reps expect a variable tied to meetings or pipeline. Design it from day one so it rewards qualified meetings, not raw activity.

A bad hire. This is the most expensive line of all. It is not the pay you lose. It is the two or three months of pipeline that never got built while you waited to see if the rep would work out, plus the time your manager spent coaching someone who was never going to get there. That is why the screen in section 04 matters more than any rate you negotiate.

When you compare options, compare the cost of getting qualified meetings, not the cost of a seat. A cheaper rep who needs constant coaching can end up costing more than a stronger one who ramps in three weeks.

03SDR, cold caller, appointment setter or AE: which seat you need

These titles get used interchangeably, and that is how companies end up with the wrong hire. Decide what the seat needs to do, then write the job around it.

SeatMain jobChannelsBest for
SDR / BDRResearch accounts, start conversations, qualify and hand over meetingsEmail, LinkedIn, phoneB2B with a clear ideal customer and a closer to hand meetings to
Cold callerHigh volume outbound calls to verified numbersPhone firstMarkets where buyers still pick up: services, local businesses, some SMB SaaS
Appointment setterBook calls for a closer from inbound or warm listsPhone, SMS, DMsCoaching, consulting and high-ticket services
Account ExecutiveRun discovery, demos and negotiation through to closeVideo calls, emailTeams that already have more pipeline than they can work

Two common mistakes. The first is hiring an AE before you have pipeline. An AE with nothing to close will start prospecting, and you will be paying closer rates for SDR work. The second is hiring a cold caller for a market that does not answer the phone. If your buyers live in their inbox and on LinkedIn, an SDR who writes well will beat a great dialer every time.

If you are not sure, start with an SDR. It is the most flexible seat, it tells you which channel works for your market, and a strong SDR is the person you will want to promote into your first AE later.

04How to test a candidate in 20 minutes

A CV tells you where someone worked. It does not tell you whether they can hold a call when a buyer pushes back in their second language. This is the screen we run at BL Agency before anyone reaches a client, and it fits in twenty minutes.

  1. Warm up (minutes 0 to 4). What role they want, why they are looking now, which US hours they can cover. Keep it short and friendly. You can do this part in Spanish if that relaxes the candidate.
  2. Numbers (minutes 4 to 8). Ask for their last closed or booked deal, flat: "Name the company and the year." Then calls per day, meetings per month, quota and attainment. Real reps know their numbers without thinking. If you get a description of their methodology instead of a name, ask once more, plainly. Habit or evasion shows on the second ask.
  3. English (minutes 8 to 15). Switch to English without announcing it. Ask them to walk you through how they would open a call with a US VP of Sales. You are listening for clarity and confidence under a little pressure, not for a perfect accent.
  4. Roleplay (minutes 15 to 18). Play a busy buyer. Push back twice: "We already have a vendor" and "Send me an email." Good reps stay calm, ask a question and keep the call alive. Weak reps either argue or give up.
  5. Close (minutes 18 to 20). Ask about compensation expectations now, not at the offer, and explain the next step.

Write your score right after the call, before you look at the CV again. Candidates who interview well but cannot name a single deal are the most common bad hire we see.

05Red flags to watch for

Most bad hires show at least one of these signs in the first conversation. None of them is an automatic no on its own, but two together usually are.

  • "Advanced English" on the CV and no English in the interview. Some candidates steer the whole call back to Spanish. Make the switch yourself and see what happens.
  • No numbers, only adjectives. "Results-driven" and "top performer" with no quota, no attainment and no deal names.
  • Only inbound or B2C experience for an outbound B2B seat. Taking calls from people who want to buy is a different job from starting conversations with people who did not ask.
  • A comp expectation far from the seat. It is not a moral failing, but it is a mismatch you want to find in minute eighteen, not after the offer.
  • A CV written for a robot. We have seen hidden instructions to AI screening tools pasted into CVs in white text. Read the CV yourself, and treat that one as a firm no.

07The first 30 days

Most of the ramp happens in the first month, and most of it depends on you, not the rep. A simple plan:

  • Week 1. Product, ideal customer and the five objections your best closer hears most. Give the rep recordings of real calls, not slide decks.
  • Week 2. Shadow and practice. The rep listens to live calls, then roleplays the same calls with your closer.
  • Week 3. First live outreach on a small list you have checked yourself. Review every message and every call recording together.
  • Week 4. Full activity with a weekly target for conversations, not just dials or emails sent.

Hold a 15 minute check-in every day for the first two weeks. It is the cheapest insurance you will buy.

08Bringing it together

Hiring a bilingual SDR from Latin America is one of the best moves a US B2B company can make when it needs more conversations without a US-sized payroll. The region works your hours, the talent pool is deep, and the savings are real. What decides whether it works is the hire itself.

  1. Decide the seat before you write the job.
  2. Budget the full cost, commission included.
  3. Run a short screen with a surprise switch to English and a live roleplay.
  4. Pick the engagement model that matches how much you want to manage.

09Frequently asked questions

Do Latin American SDRs work US hours?

Yes. Latin America sits inside the US business day, so a rep can cover Eastern, Central, Mountain or Pacific hours without working nights.

Which countries are best for hiring SDRs?

Colombia, Mexico, Peru, Argentina and Brazil all have strong pools of English-speaking sales talent. The individual matters far more than the country.

How do I test a candidate's English?

Do part of the interview in English without warning and run a short cold call roleplay where you push back. A certificate or a line on the CV tells you very little.

How long does it take to hire?

With a partner that pre-screens candidates, you can usually interview within a few days and have a rep live in two to three weeks.

Can I hire the rep directly instead of through an agency?

Yes. Many partners offer direct hire for a one-time fee, or a contractor model where they handle invoicing and payment and you can convert the rep later.

Should I hire a rep or use AI SDR agents?

It depends on your deal size and how much you want to manage. Many teams now combine both: agents do the research and drafting, and a person reviews and handles the conversations.
