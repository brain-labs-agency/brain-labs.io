Building a native-level English sales team from Latin America

By the BL Agency team14 min readNovember 3, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Native-level is about being understood and trusted on the first call, not about having no accent. Research on accented speech shows the two are different things.
  • Every Latin American country has strong English speakers. Country averages help you plan, but you hire people, so screen each candidate.
  • Test English the way the job uses it: an unplanned switch, a live roleplay with objections and a written reply to a buyer.
  • Keep the level sharp after hiring with a call library, a shared glossary of your buyer's language and weekly call reviews.

"Native-level English" shows up in a lot of job posts and a lot of search queries. For a US company building a sales team in Latin America, it usually means one thing: when a buyer in Ohio or Texas picks up the phone, the conversation should feel like any other sales call. No strain, no repeated questions, no polite exit because the buyer could not follow.

That standard is reachable, and many US companies already run sales teams from the region that meet it. But it takes a clear definition, a screen that tests the right things and a team setup that keeps the level high after the first month. This guide covers all three.

01What "native-level English" means for a sales seat

For a sales seat, native-level English means a US buyer can follow, trust and respond to the rep without extra effort. In practice that maps to the top of the standard language scales: fluent, spontaneous speech, quick understanding of implied meaning and clear writing.

The Common European Framework (CEFR) is the most widely used scale. The British Council describes a C1 speaker as someone who can speak "fluently and spontaneously" without much obvious searching for words, understand longer and more demanding texts, and recognize implicit meaning. For most sales seats, that is the real bar. C2 is closer to an educated native speaker, and it is a bonus rather than a requirement.

What C1 looks like on a sales call is more specific than any scale:

  • Speed. The rep understands a buyer who talks fast, mumbles or cuts in, and answers at the same pace.
  • Implied meaning. "We are pretty set for now" and "send me something" are not literal. The rep hears what the buyer means.
  • Natural phrasing. The rep uses the words US buyers use, not textbook English. "Does it make sense to grab 20 minutes next week?" rather than "I would like to request a meeting."
  • Writing. Emails are short, plain and correct. Most first impressions in outbound happen in writing.
  • Recovery. When the rep does not understand something, they ask in a way that sounds confident, not lost.

Notice what is not on the list: having no accent. That is the most common mistake companies make when they write "native-level" in a job post, and it filters out a lot of strong sellers.

02Accent and being understood are not the same thing

A rep can have a noticeable accent and still be completely easy to understand. Language research has shown this for more than two decades, and it is the most useful idea to bring into your hiring.

Linguists Murray Munro and Tracey Derwing separated three things that people usually lump together: accentedness (how different the speech sounds), comprehensibility (how easy it feels to understand) and intelligibility (how much is actually understood). In their studies, listeners understood most accented speech accurately, even when they rated the accent as strong. The work has since been revisited in "Foreign accent, comprehensibility and intelligibility, redux".

For a sales hire, that means the question to ask is not "does this person sound American?" It is "would my buyer have to work to follow them?" Those produce very different shortlists.

What actually makes speech hard to follow is rarely the accent itself. It is usually one of these:

  • Stress on the wrong syllable of key words, especially product or industry terms.
  • Speaking too fast when nervous, which blurs words together.
  • Flat intonation, which makes questions sound like statements and statements sound uncertain.
  • Translated phrasing that is grammatically correct but sounds odd to a US ear.

All four are coachable in weeks. A heavy accent can take years to change and usually does not need to. So screen for the four problems above, coach them where they show up, and stop treating a regional accent as a red flag. US buyers already hear many accents every day from colleagues, vendors and their own teams.

03Where to find strong English speakers in Latin America

Strong English sales talent exists in every major Latin American market, so pick a country for hours, pool size and the roles you need, then screen individuals. Country rankings are useful for planning, but they describe averages, not the person you will hire.

The most cited benchmark is the EF English Proficiency Index. In the 2025 edition, Argentina ranked 26th worldwide with a score of 575, the highest in Latin America and in EF's "high proficiency" band. Several Central and South American countries sit in the moderate band, and some of the largest markets, including Colombia and Mexico, score below the global average.

Two cautions before you read too much into the chart. First, the EF index is based on self-selected test takers, so it measures the people who chose to take an online test, not the general workforce. Second, sales teams are not hired from averages. A country with a lower average and a very large professional population can still have more strong candidates than a small country with a high average.

That second point shows up in hiring data. Near's 2026 State of LatAm Hiring Report, summarized here, found that Colombia rose to the top hiring destination for US companies, with 23% of placements, and that SDR and BDR roles were the most frequently filled roles for the second year in a row. Colombia scores below the global average on EF, yet it has a deep pool of people who have sold to US buyers for years in BPO, SaaS and fintech.

How to use this. Choose the country mix based on the hours you need covered and the size of the experienced sales pool, then let the screen decide who is native-level. The next section is how to run that screen.

04How to test English the way the job uses it

Test English by making the candidate do the job in English, not by asking about their level. A self-reported "advanced" or a certificate tells you very little about how someone handles a fast, impatient US buyer.

1. The unplanned switch. Start the interview in Spanish or Portuguese, then switch to English without warning a few minutes in. Prepared answers sound fluent; this shows you how the person thinks in English when they have not rehearsed.

2. Listen back. Play a short recording of a real sales call, ideally one with a fast or indirect buyer, and ask the candidate to summarize what the buyer wanted and what they were worried about. This tests listening and implied meaning, which is where many strong speakers still slip.

3. The live roleplay. Run a cold call or a discovery call with you as the buyer. Interrupt, push back and give a firm no. You are watching for three things: do they keep their pace, do they recover naturally, and do they still sound like themselves when it gets uncomfortable.

4. A written reply. Send a short buyer email, such as "Not a priority this quarter, maybe reach out in Q2", and give them ten minutes to answer. Look for short sentences, natural phrasing and no translated idioms.

5. A reference call. Ask a former manager, ideally a US one, how the candidate handled buyers in English. Ask specifically whether buyers ever asked to speak with someone else.

The whole screen fits in under an hour. If you are short on time, keep the roleplay. It tells you more than the other four steps combined, because it shows English, sales skill and composure in one go.

05How to structure the team around the English each seat needs

Set the English bar per seat, because an SDR, an AE and a customer success manager use English in different ways. A single "native-level" requirement for every role either filters out good people or lets weak ones through.

A few structural choices make a big difference to how the team sounds to buyers:

  • Hire a team lead early. One strong lead who coaches in English lifts the whole team faster than any course. Near's report describes one US company whose first SDR hire became team lead within three months. Plan for that path when you hire the first person.
  • Use bilingual skills on purpose. If you sell into Spanish-speaking US markets or plan to expand into Latin America, route those accounts to the reps who can work them in both languages.
  • Keep the closer close. SDRs improve fastest when they hear how the AE runs the meetings they booked. Invite them to listen in on a few each month.
  • Mix experience levels. Pair a newer rep with someone who has sold to US buyers for years. Phrasing spreads through a team faster than through any training deck.

06How to keep the level sharp after hiring

English on a sales team gets sharper or duller depending on what happens after the hire. Teams that listen to calls together and share the buyer's language improve month after month; teams that work alone tend to settle into habits.

The Bridge Group's 2025 research puts average SDR ramp at about three months. You can use that window to build English habits along with product knowledge:

  • A call library. Collect your ten best recorded calls and a few bad ones. New reps listen before they dial, and everyone adds to it.
  • A shared glossary. Write down the exact words your buyers use for their problems, your product and your competitors. Reps should say "churn" if your buyers say "churn", not "client loss."
  • Weekly call reviews. Thirty minutes, one call, with the team. Focus on one thing at a time: the opener, the objection, the close.
  • Written templates as a starting point. Give reps approved email examples for common replies, then let them adapt the wording to their own voice.
  • US context. Holidays, fiscal quarters, how titles map to seniority, why a buyer in a regulated industry answers slowly. Context is half of sounding native.

None of this is specific to Latin America. It is how good sales teams work anywhere. The difference is that a team working across a border has fewer chances to pick things up in the hallway, so you have to make those moments on purpose.

07Why hours and retention matter as much as English

A team only stays native-level if the people stay, and the biggest threat to retention is the schedule, not the work. Latin America works US business hours on a normal local day, which is a large part of why sales teams there last.

Most of the region sits between UTC-3 and UTC-6, which means a rep in Bogotá, Lima or Mexico City covers an Eastern or Central day without a night shift. Teams that work through the night elsewhere tend to lose their best people first, and with them, the English and product knowledge you spent months building.

Three things help keep a good team together:

  1. A visible path. SDR to senior SDR to team lead or AE. People stay where they can see a next step.
  2. Inclusion in the real team. Same channels, same meetings, same recognition as US colleagues. Reps who feel like an outside vendor act like one.
  3. Fair, clear variable pay. Tie it to qualified meetings or pipeline, and explain it in writing from day one.

The Bridge Group reports average SDR tenure of about 1.9 years across the US companies it surveyed. A team in Latin America with good hours, a clear path and real inclusion can match that, which means the native-level English you hired for is still on the phones a year later.

08Bringing it together

A native-level English sales team from Latin America is very achievable. It comes down to defining the bar correctly, screening for it directly and building a team that keeps getting better.

  1. Define native-level as being understood and trusted, roughly C1 or above, not as having no accent.
  2. Use country data for planning only. Every market has strong speakers; screen the person, not the passport.
  3. Test the way the job works: an unplanned switch, a listen back, a live roleplay, a written reply and a reference call.
  4. Set the English bar per seat and hire a strong team lead early.
  5. Keep the level sharp with a call library, a shared glossary, weekly call reviews and US business hours that people can sustain.

09Frequently asked questions

What does native-level English mean for a sales rep?

It means a US buyer can follow and trust the rep without extra effort. In language terms that is roughly CEFR C1 or above: fluent, spontaneous speech, quick understanding of implied meaning and clear writing. It does not mean having no accent.

Will US buyers mind a Latin American accent?

Most will not, as long as the rep is easy to understand. Research by Munro and Derwing found that listeners understand most accented speech accurately even when they rate the accent as strong. What causes trouble is usually speed, word stress or translated phrasing, and all of those are coachable.

Which Latin American country has the best English?

On the EF English Proficiency Index 2025, Argentina has the highest average in the region and ranks 26th worldwide. But averages come from self-selected test takers, and every country has strong English speakers. Colombia, for example, scores below the global average yet is the top hiring destination for US companies according to Near's 2026 report.

How do I test a sales candidate's English?

Make them do the job in English. Switch languages without warning, play a real call and ask for a summary, run a live roleplay with objections, ask for a written reply to a buyer email and check with a former manager. The roleplay alone tells you most of what you need.

Do I need C2 English for an SDR?

Usually not. C1 covers what an SDR does: short emails, cold opens, discovery questions and booking meetings. C2 is a bonus for senior AEs or roles that negotiate with executives, but requiring it for every seat filters out many strong sellers.

How long does it take a new rep to sound fully natural with my buyers?

Most of the gap closes during ramp, which the Bridge Group puts at about three months for SDRs. A call library, a glossary of your buyer's terms and weekly call reviews speed that up, because the rep learns the exact language your market uses.
