Bilingual sales reps for Spanish-speaking US markets

By the BL Agency team13 min readNovember 24, 2026

Key takeaways

  • A bilingual sales rep is useful when a real share of your buyers are more comfortable in Spanish, or when trust in your market is built in Spanish even if the deal is closed in English.
  • The opportunity is large and concentrated: about 44.9 million people speak Spanish at home in the US, and more than half live in California, Texas or Florida.
  • "Bilingual" should mean professional in both languages on a live call, including switching mid-conversation. Test both, never just the one you speak.
  • Set the rep up with Spanish materials, a shared glossary and a CRM field for language, so you can see which language actually books meetings.

Many US companies sell to buyers who speak Spanish without ever planning for it. A roofing supplier in Houston, a staffing firm in Miami, a software company selling to clinics in Los Angeles or to contractors in Phoenix: a good part of their buyers grew up speaking Spanish, run businesses where Spanish is spoken every day, or simply relax when the person on the phone can switch languages with them.

A bilingual sales rep can turn that into an advantage, but only if you hire for it on purpose. This guide covers when Spanish actually helps in US B2B sales, which markets it matters in most, what "bilingual" should mean in a job description, how to test it, and how to set the rep up so the second language turns into meetings.

01When a bilingual sales rep actually helps

A bilingual rep helps when a meaningful share of your buyers either prefer Spanish or build trust faster in it. If you cannot name the segment where that is true, a bilingual rep is a nice extra, not a strategy.

Three situations come up again and again in US B2B:

  • Your buyers run Spanish-speaking businesses. Owners and managers in trades, construction, logistics, food service, cleaning, landscaping, retail and many local services often run their day in Spanish. Even when they speak English well, a call in Spanish feels like a call from someone who understands their world.
  • The decision maker is bilingual, but the team is not. The owner may read your email in English, but the office manager who books the meeting, or the crew lead who will use the product, prefers Spanish. A rep who can talk to both moves the deal along faster.
  • You sell across the Americas from the US. Companies with buyers in Puerto Rico, Mexico or the rest of Latin America need Spanish for the conversation, even if the contract is in English.

It helps less than people expect when your buyers are mostly corporate teams who work in English all day, or when Spanish would only matter for a handful of accounts. In those cases a strong English-speaking rep with good research beats a bilingual rep with average skills.

A simple test: look at your last 50 closed deals and your current pipeline. Count the accounts where someone on the buyer side spoke Spanish to your team, asked for materials in Spanish or would have preferred it. That count tells you more than any market report.

02How big the Spanish-speaking market is, and where it is

It is large and geographically concentrated. About 44.9 million people age 5 and older spoke Spanish at home in the US in 2024, roughly one in seven, and more than half of them live in California, Texas or Florida.

The broader picture comes from the Census Bureau: the Hispanic population reached 68 million as of July 2024, about 20% of the US population, and 15 states had a million or more Hispanic residents. According to USAFacts, using Census data, the number of people who speak Spanish at home grew 21.3% between 2010 and 2024, almost twice the growth of the population overall.

Metro areas matter more than states for sales planning. The same USAFacts data shows the largest Spanish-speaking metro populations in Los Angeles, New York and Miami, where about 43% of residents age 5 and older speak Spanish at home. If your territory includes those metros, or cities like Houston, San Antonio, Phoenix or Dallas, Spanish will come up on your calls whether you plan for it or not.

The business side is growing too. Stanford's 2025 State of Latino Entrepreneurship report counted 465,202 Latino-owned businesses in 2023, up 44% from 2018, while white-owned businesses slightly declined over the same period. Those are companies that buy software, services, equipment and staff, and many of them are run by owners who switch between English and Spanish all day.

03Most Spanish-speaking buyers also speak English. That is the point

Most of your Spanish-speaking buyers also speak English, so a bilingual rep is rarely about translation. It is about comfort, speed and trust, and about being able to follow the buyer into whichever language they choose.

The data backs this up. In 2024, 58.9% of people who speak Spanish at home told the Census Bureau they speak English "very well", according to USAFacts. Language use also shifts across generations. In Pew Research Center's 2023 report on Latinos and Spanish, 75% of Latino adults said they can carry on a conversation in Spanish pretty well or very well, but only 34% of third or higher generation Latinos said the same. At the same time, 85% said it is at least somewhat important for future generations to speak Spanish.

For a sales team, that adds up to three practical rules:

  • Open in English unless you know better. Default to English in the first email and the first seconds of a call, and offer Spanish naturally, for example "también podemos hablar en español si prefiere".
  • Follow the buyer. If the buyer switches, switch with them. Many bilingual conversations mix both languages, and a rep who stays stiffly in one can sound distant.
  • Never assume from a name. A Spanish surname tells you nothing reliable about a person's preferred language. Assuming can come across as careless at best and offensive at worst.

That last point is why language skill is not enough on its own. A good bilingual rep reads the conversation, not the contact record.

04Matching the rep to the market

Match the rep to the market by making sure they understand the Spanish your buyers speak and sound respectful in it. They do not need to share the accent, but they need to understand it without effort and adjust their tone to the region.

Spanish in the US is not one thing. A call to a contractor in San Antonio, a clinic owner in Miami and a distributor in the Bronx can sound like three different conversations. Vocabulary, speed, formality and even the polite way to open a call change from place to place.

In practice, a few habits travel well across all of these markets:

  • Start formal. Use "usted" with a new business contact until the buyer signals otherwise. Moving to "tú" too early is a common mistake, especially with older owners.
  • Use neutral business Spanish in writing. Emails and materials should avoid slang from any one country, so they read well everywhere.
  • Keep your product terms consistent. Decide which Spanish words you use for your product, its features and your common objections, and keep them the same across every rep and every document.

05What "bilingual" should mean in the job description

"Bilingual" should mean the rep can run a full sales conversation in either language, at a professional level, and switch between them without losing the thread. Anything less is "speaks some Spanish", which is useful but is a different job.

Most job posts just say "bilingual English/Spanish preferred". That attracts candidates who learned Spanish in school, candidates who speak it at home but have never sold in it, and candidates who are strong in Spanish but hesitant in English. Be specific instead:

  • Spoken English: can handle objections, small talk and a fast US buyer on a cold call without notes.
  • Spoken Spanish: the same, with business vocabulary and a respectful register.
  • Written English and Spanish: can write a short, clear cold email and a CRM note in each language, with no grammar errors.
  • Switching: can move between languages mid-call when the buyer does.
  • Market knowledge: has sold to, or worked with, the kind of buyer you sell to, ideally in the regions you cover.

Then say which language the rep will use most. A role that is 80% English with Spanish when needed needs a different hire than one that is mostly Spanish calls to small business owners. Being honest about the mix saves both sides time.

06How to test both languages in a short screen

Test both languages the way the job uses them: live, on a call, with a buyer who pushes back. A conversation about the candidate's experience tells you how they talk about themselves, not how they sell.

  1. English roleplay. You play a busy US buyer. The candidate opens, handles one objection and asks for a meeting.
  2. Spanish roleplay. Someone fluent plays a Spanish-speaking owner. Same goal, different buyer.
  3. The switch. In one of the calls, the buyer changes language halfway through. Watch whether the candidate follows smoothly and keeps control of the conversation.
  4. Two short emails. One cold email in each language, to a buyer you describe. Look for clarity, tone and correct grammar.
  5. A debrief note. A three line CRM note in English about the Spanish call. This is the handoff your account executives will read.

If nobody on your team speaks Spanish well, do not skip the Spanish part. Ask a trusted bilingual colleague, an advisor or a recruiter to run it. 07Setting a bilingual rep up to succeed

Set a bilingual rep up with materials in both languages, a clear rule for when to use each one, and a way to measure results by language. Without those, the second language stays a personal skill instead of becoming part of your sales process.

  • Spanish materials. At minimum: a one page overview, a short case or product sheet (with real, approved content only) and email templates. Have a native speaker review them. Machine translation is a starting point, not a finished document.
  • A shared glossary. A short list of how you say your product, your features and your common objections in Spanish, so every rep and every document uses the same words.
  • A language field in the CRM. Record the language each conversation happened in. After a quarter, you will know whether Spanish calls book more meetings, hold more of them or close faster in your market.
  • A handoff plan. If your account executives only speak English, decide in advance how a Spanish-preferring buyer moves to the next stage: a bilingual AE, the SDR joining the first call, or materials in Spanish ahead of the meeting.
  • Onboarding in both languages. Let the rep practice the pitch in Spanish as well as English during the first weeks. Our SDR onboarding plan shows a 30, 60 and 90 day shape you can adapt.

The handoff is where many teams lose the benefit. A buyer who had a warm conversation in Spanish and then meets an AE who cannot follow them often goes cold. Plan that step before the first meeting is booked.

08Bringing it together

A bilingual sales rep is one of the simplest ways for a US company to stand out in markets where Spanish is part of everyday business. The opportunity is real and concentrated in a handful of states and metros, but it only pays off when you hire for it deliberately and build it into your process.

  1. Confirm the need: count the deals and pipeline accounts where Spanish already came up.
  2. Know where your buyers are: California, Texas, Florida and the big metros hold most of the Spanish-speaking market.
  3. Remember most buyers also speak English: the rep's job is to follow the buyer, not to translate.
  4. Match the rep to the market: understanding regional Spanish and using a respectful register matter more than accent.
  5. Define bilingual clearly: professional in both languages, on calls and in writing, including the switch.
  6. Test both languages live: never hire a bilingual rep after testing only one.
  7. Build it into the process: Spanish materials, a glossary, a CRM language field and a planned handoff.

09Frequently asked questions

What is a bilingual sales rep?

A sales professional who can run a full sales conversation in two languages, in this case English and Spanish, at a professional level on calls and in writing, and switch between them when the buyer does.

Do I need a bilingual SDR to sell to Hispanic-owned businesses in the US?

Not always. Many owners speak English well, but a rep who can switch to Spanish often builds trust faster and can talk to the whole team, not just the decision maker. Check how often Spanish already comes up in your deals before deciding.

Which US markets benefit most from Spanish-speaking sales reps?

California, Texas and Florida hold more than half of the 44.9 million people who speak Spanish at home. Metros like Los Angeles, New York, Miami, Houston, San Antonio and Phoenix are where Spanish comes up most on sales calls.

Should a bilingual rep open calls in Spanish?

Usually not. Default to English, offer Spanish naturally, and switch if the buyer does. Never assume someone prefers Spanish because of their name.

How do I test a candidate's Spanish if I do not speak it?

Ask a fluent colleague, advisor or recruiter to run a short Spanish roleplay and review a cold email written in Spanish. Skipping the Spanish test is the most common way this hire goes wrong.

Does the accent or country of the rep matter?

Less than people think. What matters is that the rep understands the Spanish your buyers speak without effort, uses a respectful register such as "usted" with new contacts, and writes in neutral business Spanish.
